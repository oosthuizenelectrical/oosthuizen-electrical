Where to charge an electric car in Cape Town: public chargers vs charging at home

How to find a public charger near you, what it costs per kWh in 2026, and how that compares with charging at home or from your own solar.

"Where can I charge my car in Cape Town?" is one of the first questions new EV owners ask us. The short answer: there are plenty of public chargers in the city, at malls, dealerships, petrol stations and along the N1 and N2. But most owners end up doing nearly all their charging at home, because it is cheaper and more convenient. This guide covers both.

How to find a public charger in Cape Town

Chargers open, move and go offline all the time, so the most reliable way to find one is a live map rather than a printed list. These are the ones Cape Town drivers use most:

  • Google Maps. Search "EV charger" and you will see nearby stations. GridCars, the biggest network, lists its stations on Google Maps.
  • PlugShare. A free app with stations from every network, plus check-ins and photos from other drivers, so you can see whether a charger was working recently.
  • Charge Pocket (GridCars). GridCars manages most of South Africa's public chargers. Its app shows live status and lets you pay. Search "Charge Pocket" in your app store.
  • Rubicon Charge. Rubicon runs the second large network. Many of its DC fast chargers now take tap-to-pay with a normal bank card, and Rubicon and GridCars cards work on each other's stations.
  • AutoTrader's live map. A quick web map showing which stations are online or offline.

For context: South Africa has roughly 550 to 700 public charging stations, depending on who is counting, and the number is growing every year.

Examples of public fast chargers around Cape Town

Always check the app before you drive there, but these are some of the better-known fast-charging spots:

  • Century City and Tygervalley (both near the N1), and Somerset Mall (N2): high-power rapid chargers installed with Audi.
  • V&A Waterfront: a 24/7 charging site on Port Road that you can pay for with a bank card.
  • Richmond Park: a solar- and battery-powered hub with four 120 kW DC fast chargers, which opened in 2026 at a pilot price well below the usual public rate.
  • Many car dealerships, shopping centres and fuel stations on the main routes also have AC or DC chargers. Your car's own app or brand card will often show these.

What does public charging cost?

Public charging is priced per kWh (the unit of energy that goes into your battery). Typical prices in late 2026:

  • DC fast charging: about R7.00 to R8.45 per kWh. Rubicon charges a flat R7.00 with its card; GridCars ranges from about R7.35 to R8.45.
  • AC (slower) public chargers: usually cheaper, from about R5.90 per kWh.
  • Solar-powered pilot sites such as Richmond Park have been charging about R3.49 per kWh, but that is a pilot price and may change.

Fast chargers are brilliant for road trips and top-ups. As your only way to charge, they get expensive.

Public vs home vs solar: a worked example

Take a typical mid-size EV using about 17.5 kWh per 100 km, driven 1,500 km a month. That is around 262 kWh a month. Here is roughly what that costs, depending on where you charge:

Where you chargePrice per kWhCost for 1,500 km
Public fast chargerR7.00–R8.45R1,840–R2,220
Home, City prepaidR4.14–R4.94R1,090–R1,300
Home, mostly solarunder R1a fraction of that

Home figures use the City of Cape Town 2026/27 Domestic tariff incl. VAT: 413.79c/kWh up to 600 kWh a month and 493.90c/kWh above that. Adding an EV often pushes a household over 600 kWh, so the higher rate is the more realistic one. Eskom-supplied areas have different tariffs. Your car's real consumption depends on the model, speed and how you drive.

On these numbers, charging at home instead of at public fast chargers saves somewhere between about R550 and R1,100 a month. The more you drive, the bigger the difference. To compare with what you spend on petrol now, try the fuel savings calculator on our EV charging page.

Is there a cheaper time of day to charge at home?

The City of Cape Town has a residential Time-of-Use tariff, with off-peak electricity at about R3.01 per kWh incl. VAT. But it comes with fixed monthly charges of several hundred rand, so it only pays off for households that use a lot of electricity. Most EV owners on prepaid are better off staying on the Domestic tariff and, if they have solar, charging during the day.

Charging from solar

If you have solar panels, the cheapest kWh you can put in your car is one your roof made. A wall charger can be set up so the car charges mainly when the sun is shining, for example while you work from home or on weekends. How much of your charging solar can cover depends on the size of your array, your inverter and your battery, and on when the car is actually parked at home. We look at your system first and tell you honestly what to expect. See our solar and backup page and the solar cost guide.

Do I need a wall charger, or can I use a normal plug?

The portable cable that comes with most EVs plugs into a normal wall socket, but it is slow (often only about 10 to 15 km of range per hour) and puts a long, heavy load on a circuit that was not designed for it. A wall charger on its own dedicated circuit, at around 7 kW, adds roughly 40 km of range per hour, so a car is full by morning even after a long day. Quick answers

Are public chargers in Cape Town free?

Very few are. A handful of shopping centres and businesses offer free or discounted charging to customers, but most public chargers charge per kWh, and some add a fee if you stay plugged in after the car is full.

Do I need an app or a card to use a public charger?

Usually one of them. A growing number of fast chargers now accept tap-to-pay with a bank card. Otherwise a GridCars or Rubicon card or app works across both networks.

Does load shedding stop public chargers?

Some sites have backup power or solar and batteries; many do not. Check live status in the app before you go. At home, a charger can be wired to work with a solar and battery system.
